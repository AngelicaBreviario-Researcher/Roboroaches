Rise of the Roboroaches

Cyborg invertebrates challenge validity and necessitate stronger ethical standards

Angelica Breviario1*, Bob Fischer2, Thomas E. White3, Kate Lynch1

  • 1 School of History and Philosophy of Science, The University of Sydney, Australia
  • 2 Department of Philosophy, Texas State University, USA
  • 3 School of Life and Environmental Sciences, The University of Sydney, Australia

* Contact: angelica.breviario@sydney.edu.au

Poster presented at the Biennial Meeting of the Philosophy of Science Association, San Francisco, November 2026.

In one line

As currently implemented, insect models in bioelectronics offer low validity at a potentially high welfare cost.

Abstract

Invertebrates, particularly insects, are increasingly proposed as models for bioelectronics and neural engineering, including for preclinical testing of devices intended for human use. They are typically presented as cheap, accessible, and ethically unproblematic, since most jurisdictions exclude them from animal welfare regulation. We challenge both halves of this justification. On validity, we argue that the assumption that invertebrates are simpler versions of mammals lacks support from evolutionary biology and theories of biological complexity. We also show that the disanalogies between insect and human cardiac and neural systems are often acknowledged by the same researchers who propose these models. On ethics, we review the growing evidence that insects have welfare-relevant capacities, including pain, sufficient to warrant precaution under the 3Rs. Yet proposed and published procedures routinely omit anaesthesia, welfare monitoring, humane endpoints and efforts to reduce animal numbers. Taken together, these points invert the usual rationale: as currently implemented, insect models in bioelectronics offer low validity at a potentially high welfare cost. We do not argue against invertebrate use. We argue that it should be justified case by case, with model validity informing expected benefit, and with the 3Rs applied as they would be for protected species. In the absence of formal oversight, the responsibility for making these judgements rests largely with researchers, and we outline practical steps for meeting it.
